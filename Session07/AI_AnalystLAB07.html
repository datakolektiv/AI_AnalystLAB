7.1 The business case¶

A city bike-sharing operator wants to plan capacity. If they can understand what drives hourly demand — temperature, humidity, wind, whether it is a working day — they can position bikes where and when they are needed, and explain their decisions to the city with numbers.

The outcome they care about is a count: the number of bikes rented in a given hour (cnt). That single fact — the thing we predict is a count — is what sends us to a new tool. You cannot rent half a bike, you cannot rent a negative number of bikes, and quiet hours sit at a very different level from rush hours. A model for counts has to respect all of that.

7.4 Why a count is different — and why ordinary regression is the wrong tool¶

Let us first see what a count looks like. We draw a histogram of the hourly rentals:

Notice three things a count always does and a straight-line model ignores:

  • the values are whole numbers (you cannot rent 12.4 bikes),
  • they are never negative (the floor is 0),
  • the spread is lopsided — a long tail of busy hours, piled up against a hard wall at zero.

Now watch the ordinary linear regression from Session 04 run straight into that wall. We fit it to our four drivers and ask for its smallest prediction:

A negative number of bikes. The model has done exactly what a straight line must do — kept going down past zero — and produced an answer that cannot exist. It also assumes the spread of outcomes is the same everywhere, which is plainly false here (quiet 3 a.m. hours vary far less than rush hours). Ordinary regression is the wrong shape for a count. We need a model that is built to stay positive and to treat counts as counts.

7.5 A short refresher — the Poisson distribution¶

Back in Session 01 you met the Poisson distribution: the natural description of "how many independent events happen in a fixed window" — calls per minute, arrivals per hour, rentals per hour. It has a single number, the rate λ ("lambda"), which is its average count.

Its probability formula tells us how likely each possible count is. If the average rate is λ, the chance of seeing exactly $k$ events in the window is

$$P(X = k) = \frac{\lambda^{k}\, e^{-\lambda}}{k!}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

Read one piece at a time, it is friendlier than it looks:

  • $P(X = k)$ — the probability of exactly $k$ events (exactly 0 rentals, or exactly 1, or exactly 2, …);
  • $\lambda$ — the average count, i.e. the rate; a bigger λ slides the whole distribution to the right;
  • $k!$ — "$k$ factorial", meaning $k \times (k-1) \times \dots \times 1$ (and $0! = 1$ by convention);
  • $e^{-\lambda}$ — a shrinking factor (the exponential from Review 05) that keeps all the probabilities adding up to exactly 1.

(Because a count is a whole number, this formula gives a probability for each separate value and is called a probability mass function (p.m.f.). Its continuous cousin — for a quantity that can land on any value, like a height — is a probability density function (p.d.f.).)

The one property to carry into this session is the distribution's signature: its variance equals its mean — both are λ. A Poisson with an average of 5 also has a spread (variance) of 5. Here is its shape for two rates, with one probability worked out by hand so the formula feels concrete:

In [5]:
k = np.arange(0, 16)                         # possible counts: 0, 1, 2, ...
for lam in [2, 5]:
    plt.plot(k, stats.poisson.pmf(k, lam), "o-", label=f"λ = {lam}")
plt.title("The Poisson distribution of counts  (mean = variance = λ)")
plt.xlabel("count k"); plt.ylabel("probability"); plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

# Check the probability formula by hand against SciPy: exactly k = 3 events when the rate is λ = 2.
lam, k_example = 2, 3
by_hand = lam**k_example * np.exp(-lam) / np.prod(np.arange(1, k_example + 1))   # λ^k · e^(−λ) / k!
print(f"P(exactly {k_example} events, λ = {lam}) — by the formula: {by_hand:.4f}")
print(f"P(exactly {k_example} events, λ = {lam}) — from SciPy    : {stats.poisson.pmf(k_example, lam):.4f}")

P(exactly 3 events, λ = 2) — by the formula: 0.1804
P(exactly 3 events, λ = 2) — from SciPy    : 0.1804

Keep that equality — mean = variance — in the back of your mind. It is the assumption Poisson regression is built on, and later in this session our data will break it, which is exactly where the negative binomial model comes in.

7.6 The Poisson regression model — and the log link¶

Poisson regression predicts the rate λ (the expected count) from our drivers. But there is a catch we already saw: a count's rate must stay positive, while a straight line $\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots$ happily goes negative. How do we connect a must-stay-positive rate to a can-go-anywhere straight line?

With a logarithm — exactly the tool from Review 05. Instead of modelling λ directly, Poisson regression models its log:

$$\ln(\lambda) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \dots$$

and to get the rate back, it undoes the log with the exponential:

$$\lambda = e^{\,\beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \dots}$$

This is the log link, and it is the whole reason Review 05 existed. Because $e^{(\text{anything})}$ is always positive (R5.5), the predicted rate can never fall below zero, no matter what the straight line does.

One rate per row — from just a few numbers. Here is a subtle but important shift from Session 01, and it is worth pausing on. There, the Poisson distribution had a single rate λ for the whole process. In Poisson regression, every row gets its own rate. Feed one hour's temperature, humidity, wind and working-day flag into the formula and out comes that hour's expected count — which we write $\lambda_i$, where the little $i$ just labels the row. A warm working-day hour produces a large $\lambda_i$; a cold, windy night produces a small one. With roughly 17,000 rows, there are, in effect, 17,000 different rates.

But look closely at what we actually estimate: not those thousands of rates, only the handful of coefficients $\beta_0, \beta_1, \dots$ — in our model, just five numbers (the intercept plus one per driver). Those five shared numbers generate a rate for every single row. That economy — a few coefficients, one rate per observation — is the whole idea of regression, and you will see it concretely in §7.9, where the same fitted model hands one hour a high expected count and another a low one. (Session 05 did exactly this: each customer got their own probability from one shared set of coefficients, via the sigmoid instead of the exponential.)

The coefficients are found by maximum likelihood — the "make the observed data most probable" recipe from Review 04 and Session 05. Same machinery, new model.

7.8 Reading the coefficients — rate ratios¶

In Session 05 you turned a logistic coefficient into a plain-English odds ratio by taking $e^{\beta}$. Poisson regression has the exact same move, and the result is called a rate ratio: how much the expected count is multiplied when a driver goes up by one unit. (Why multiplied, not added? Because of the log link: adding to the log of the rate multiplies the rate itself — $e^{a+\beta}=e^{a}\cdot e^{\beta}$, straight from Review 05.)

We exponentiate the coefficients:

Now each number reads as a sentence. A rate ratio of, say, 1.8 means "this multiplies the expected number of rentals by 1.8" (an 80% increase); a rate ratio of 0.6 means "this multiplies it by 0.6" (a 40% drop). Because temp, hum, and windspeed run on a 0-to-1 scale, their rate ratios compare the lowest value to the highest — e.g. the temp ratio is the demand multiplier going from the coldest hour to the warmest. For workingday, which is simply 0 or 1, the ratio is the straightforward "working day vs. not" multiplier. This is the table you would actually put in front of the operations team.

One honest footnote, kept short because our data does not need it here. Every row in this dataset covers the same one-hour window, so the counts are directly comparable. But sometimes the windows differ — one shop is observed for a month, another for a week; one region has 10,000 customers, another 2,000. Comparing raw counts would then be unfair. The standard fix is an offset: you add log(exposure) (log of the window length, or the population size) into the model as a fixed term, which turns the prediction into a rate per unit of exposure rather than a raw count. You will not need it today — just recognise the word offset when you meet it.

7.10 The crack — overdispersion¶

Now we return to the warning from Section 7.3. Poisson regression inherits the Poisson distribution's signature assumption: at any given rate, the variance equals the mean. Real counts very often refuse to obey — their spread is far larger than their average. This is called overdispersion, and it is the single most common thing that goes wrong with a Poisson model.

We can capture it in a single number, the dispersion statistic. It asks, for every hour, how far the real count landed from the rate the model predicted, and compares that to the spread a Poisson promises at that rate. Writing $\hat{\mu}_i$ for the predicted rate of row $i$ (the $\lambda_i$ of §7.6), $n$ for the number of rows and $p$ for the number of coefficients, it is

$$\hat{\phi} \;=\; \frac{1}{\,n - p\,}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left(y_i - \hat{\mu}_i\right)^2}{\hat{\mu}_i}$$

One piece at a time:

  • $\left(y_i - \hat{\mu}_i\right)^2$ — how far the real count $y_i$ fell from its predicted rate $\hat{\mu}_i$, squared so that over- and under-shoots both count as "off";
  • $\div\,\hat{\mu}_i$ — we divide by the predicted rate because, for a Poisson, the variance equals the mean $\hat{\mu}_i$; this scales every gap by the spread we would expect, so a busy rush hour and a quiet night are judged on the same footing. (Each scaled gap is called a squared Pearson residual.)
  • $\frac{1}{n-p}\sum$ — average those scaled gaps over the degrees of freedom (rows minus coefficients).

Under a true Poisson, each scaled gap averages about 1, so $\hat{\phi} \approx 1$. If the counts scatter more than a Poisson allows, the gaps swell and $\hat{\phi}$ climbs well above 1. Let us compute it:

In [9]:
mu = poisson.predict(X)                                   # the rate the model expects for each hour
dispersion = ((y - mu)**2 / mu).sum() / (len(y) - X.shape[1])
print("Dispersion statistic:", round(dispersion, 1))
print("For a well-behaved Poisson it should be about 1.")

Dispersion statistic: 134.9
For a well-behaved Poisson it should be about 1.

Far, far above 1. Our hourly rentals are badly overdispersed — their real variability dwarfs what a Poisson allows. Why does this matter? It is not that the coefficients are wrong; it is that the Poisson model, trusting its mean-equals-variance assumption, thinks it has far more certainty than it really does. It reports standard errors that are too small, which makes p-values look tiny and drivers look more "significant" than the evidence warrants. An honest analyst cannot ship inference from an overdispersed Poisson. We need a model that allows the spread to be bigger than the mean.

A number like that is convincing, but let us see it with a small numerical simulation. We ask the fitted Poisson model to generate its own pretend dataset: for each hour, we draw one random count from a Poisson whose rate is that hour's prediction $\hat{\mu}_i$. If the Poisson assumption were true, the real counts would scatter exactly like this simulated "Poisson world."

For every hour we then compute its Pearson residual — the scaled gap $\left(y_i - \hat{\mu}_i\right)/\sqrt{\hat{\mu}_i}$ from the formula above — and put the real residuals next to the simulated ones:

In [10]:
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)

mu_arr = mu.to_numpy()                                    # the model's predicted rate for each hour
y_arr  = y.to_numpy()                                     # the real count for each hour

# Pearson residual of the REAL data: how far off we were, in the units a true Poisson calls normal.
pearson_real = (y_arr - mu_arr) / np.sqrt(mu_arr)

# The simulation: let the fitted Poisson GENERATE a count for each hour (a random draw from a
# Poisson with that hour's predicted rate) — the "Poisson world" the model believes in.
y_poisson_world = rng.poisson(mu_arr)
pearson_sim = (y_poisson_world - mu_arr) / np.sqrt(mu_arr)

lim = np.percentile(np.abs(pearson_real), 99)            # frame the bulk of the real residuals
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4), sharex=True)

axes[1].hist(pearson_real, bins=60, range=(-lim, lim), color="steelblue", edgecolor="white")

The two pictures could hardly differ more. In the Poisson world the residuals huddle tightly around zero, with a spread of about 1 — exactly what a Poisson promises. The real residuals sprawl far wider, with a spread of about 11 — and $11^{2} \approx 135$, precisely the dispersion statistic we just computed. The picture and the number tell one story: the real counts swing far more violently than any Poisson could produce. That is overdispersion — and it is why we now reach for a model built to allow it.

7.11 The fix — Negative Binomial regression¶

The negative binomial model is the standard remedy for overdispersion. The cleanest way to picture it: it is a Poisson regression with one extra dial — a dispersion parameter, usually called α ("alpha") — that lets the variance grow larger than the mean. Turn the dial to zero and you are back to Poisson; turn it up and the model permits more and more extra spread. Everything else — the log link, the rate ratios, maximum likelihood — stays exactly as before.

Where the distribution comes from. A Poisson regression assumes that two hours with the same temperature, humidity, wind and working-day flag share the exact same rate μ. That is too strict: countless things we never measured — a concert downtown, a passing drizzle, a local holiday mood — nudge the true rate up or down. The negative binomial embraces this. It says the rate for each hour is itself uncertain, wobbling around the predicted μ, and only then is the count drawn as a Poisson. Blending a Poisson with a (gamma-distributed) wandering rate in this way is exactly what produces the negative binomial distribution — a count distribution with built-in room for extra spread. (For this reason it is also called a gamma–Poisson mixture.)

Its probability formula. If a count $Y$ has mean $\mu$ and dispersion $\alpha$, then — writing $r = 1/\alpha$ — the probability of exactly $k$ events is

$$P(Y = k) = \frac{\Gamma(k + r)}{\Gamma(r)\,k!}\left(\frac{r}{r + \mu}\right)^{\!r}\left(\frac{\mu}{r + \mu}\right)^{\!k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

This is a probability mass function again (here $\Gamma$ is the gamma function, a smooth stand-in for the factorial that lets $r$ take any positive value). You need not memorise it — the two numbers that run it are the whole story, and both connect straight to the regression:

  • $\mu$ — the mean, i.e. the rate. This is exactly what Poisson regression already modelled. In negative binomial regression we set $\ln(\mu_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \dots$ — the same log link, the same coefficients, and therefore the same rate ratios $e^{\beta}$ (§7.8). One $\mu_i$ per row, built from a handful of shared coefficients (§7.6), completely unchanged.
  • $\alpha$ — the dispersion, our "extra-spread dial". It fixes how far the variance may exceed the mean, through one clean rule:

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mu + \alpha\,\mu^{2}.$$

At $\alpha = 0$ this collapses to $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mu$ — the Poisson's mean-equals-variance, recovered exactly; the bigger $\alpha$, the more extra spread the $\alpha\mu^{2}$ term adds. And crucially, unlike $\mu$, there is one single $\alpha$ for the whole model — a global number answering "how overdispersed is this data?". (The $r = 1/\alpha$ above is the classical "number of successes" parameter; a huge $r$ — i.e. a tiny $\alpha$ — is indistinguishable from a Poisson.)

So negative binomial regression is simply Poisson regression's mean model plus one dispersion number: the β's still set each row's rate $\mu_i$ and its rate ratios, while the single α soaks up the extra variability that was breaking the Poisson. We fit it with sm.NegativeBinomial, then put the two models side by side to see what changes:

7.12 Choosing between them — the likelihood-ratio test¶

"Clearly not zero" is a judgement call; let us make it a formal test. Each model reports a maximised log-likelihood — call it $\ell = \ln L$ — a single number saying how probable the observed counts are under that model (the maximum-likelihood idea of Review 04). The likelihood-ratio (LR) test compares the two by twice the gap between their log-likelihoods:

$$\mathrm{LR} \;=\; 2\left(\ell_{\text{NB}} - \ell_{\text{Poisson}}\right) \;\sim\; \chi^{2}_{\,k},$$

where $k$ is the number of extra parameters the richer model carries — here $k = 1$, the single dispersion α. If that extra α truly earns its keep, the negative binomial's log-likelihood sits much higher, the LR statistic is large, and the chi-square distribution turns it into a tiny p-value.

But the LR test has one strict requirement: the two models must be nested. Two models are nested when the simpler one is a special case of the richer one — when you can obtain it exactly, by fixing one or more of the richer model's parameters to a set value. That is precisely our situation: the Poisson is the negative binomial with α forced to 0 (turn the extra-spread dial fully off, and the negative binomial becomes a Poisson). Because the simpler model lives inside the richer one, the test can ask a clean question — "does freeing α improve the fit by more than luck?" — and the $\chi^{2}_{k}$ approximation, with $k$ equal to the number of freed parameters, holds.

When two models are not nested — neither is a restriction of the other (say, two models with different predictors, or a zero-inflated model versus a plain one) — the LR test does not apply; you compare them with information criteria such as AIC or BIC instead. Here, happily, we are nested, so the LR test is exactly the right tool. Let us run it:

In [12]:
LR_statistic = 2 * (negbin.llf - poisson.llf)             # twice the gap in log-likelihood
p_value = stats.chi2.sf(LR_statistic, df=1)               # 1 extra parameter (alpha)

print("Likelihood-ratio statistic (Poisson vs Negative Binomial):", round(LR_statistic, 1))
print("p-value:", p_value)

Likelihood-ratio statistic (Poisson vs Negative Binomial): 2056396.5
p-value: 0.0

A gigantic statistic and a p-value that is effectively zero: the negative binomial fits significantly better, exactly as the overdispersion diagnostic warned.

(A technical fine point, for completeness: because α = 0 sits on the very edge of its allowed range — α can never be negative — the exact reference distribution is a 50:50 blend of $\chi^{2}_{0}$ and $\chi^{2}_{1}$, which simply halves the p-value. Here it is "essentially zero" either way, so the verdict is unchanged.)

So the decision rule for your own work is simple:

  • variance ≈ mean (dispersion near 1) → Poisson is fine;
  • variance ≫ mean (dispersion well above 1, LR test significant) → use the negative binomial.

One pointer, not a detour. Occasionally a count has far more zeros than even the negative binomial expects (think: most customers file no claims at all). There is a further variant, zero-inflated regression, for exactly that case. We will not cover it here — just know the name exists for the day you meet a mountain of zeros.

Hourly bike demand is driven mainly by the weather: warmth lifts it sharply, while humidity and wind hold it back.

7.16 Recap, and a look ahead to Session 08¶

Today you added the third regression to your toolkit:

  • a count needs its own model, because ordinary regression predicts impossible negative, fractional values;
  • Poisson regression uses the log link (Review 05) to keep the predicted rate positive, and its coefficients read as rate ratios $e^{\beta}$ — the count cousin of Session 05's odds ratios;
  • real counts are usually overdispersed (variance ≫ mean), which makes a Poisson model falsely overconfident;
  • the negative binomial model fixes this with one extra dispersion parameter, and the likelihood-ratio test confirms when it is needed;
  • and, as always, Python computed the numbers and chose the model — Claude only drafted the words.
