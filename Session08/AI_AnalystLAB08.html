8.4 A reminder from Review 03 — entropy is uncertainty¶

Session 08 leans on one idea from Review 03 (Information Theory): entropy. You do not need to re-read the whole review, just to recall the intuition, because it is the engine inside every classification tree.

The picture says it all. We want to predict the next ball drawn from an urn. Urn A is 8 black to 2 white — fairly predictable, low uncertainty. Urn B is 5 black to 5 white — a coin toss, maximum uncertainty. Entropy is simply the number that measures this: low when a group is "pure" (mostly one class), high when it is a mixed 50/50 muddle.

Here is entropy computed directly from a list of labels — exactly the Review 03 formula $H = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i$, now reading the probabilities straight off the data:

In [25]:
def entropy(labels):
    # Shannon entropy (in bits) of a set of class labels.
    labels = np.asarray(labels)
    _, counts = np.unique(labels, return_counts=True)
    p = counts / counts.sum()          # the proportion of each class
    return float(-np.sum(p * np.log2(p)))

# Urn A (8 black, 2 white) vs Urn B (5 black, 5 white):
urn_a = ["black"] * 8 + ["white"] * 2
urn_b = ["black"] * 5 + ["white"] * 5
print("Urn A  (8:2)  entropy =", round(entropy(urn_a), 3), "bits   <- more predictable")
print("Urn B  (5:5)  entropy =", round(entropy(urn_b), 3), "bits   <- maximally uncertain")
# A perfectly pure group has zero entropy:
print("Pure   (10:0) entropy =", round(entropy(["black"] * 10), 3), "bits   <- no uncertainty at all")

Urn A  (8:2)  entropy = 0.722 bits   <- more predictable
Urn B  (5:5)  entropy = 1.0 bits   <- maximally uncertain
Pure   (10:0) entropy = -0.0 bits   <- no uncertainty at all

8.5 What is a decision tree?¶

A decision tree is a flowchart of yes/no questions. You start at the top, answer one question at a time, and follow the branches down until you reach a leaf that gives you a prediction. Here is a toy tree for "should I buy this used car?":

Three pieces of vocabulary, and that is the whole model:

  • Root node (top): the first question asked of every case — here, "Red car?"
  • Internal nodes: further questions that narrow things down — "Model > 2010?", "Mileage > 50 000 km?"
  • Leaves (bottom): the final answers — Buy / Don't buy.

Every path from the root to a leaf is a plain-English if-then rule: "IF the car is red AND newer than 2010, THEN buy." That readability is the entire point. The only real question left is: how does the tree decide which question to ask, and in what order? That is where entropy comes back.

8.6 How a tree chooses a split — Information Gain¶

A tree builds itself greedily: at every node it tries all possible questions and picks the one that makes the resulting groups as pure as possible. "Pure" means low entropy. The improvement a split gives us has a name — Information Gain:

$$\text{Information Gain} = \underbrace{H(\text{parent})}_{\text{uncertainty before}} \;-\; \underbrace{\left[\, w_L\,H(\text{left}) + w_R\,H(\text{right}) \,\right]}_{\text{average uncertainty after}}$$

where $w_L, w_R$ are the fractions of cases sent left and right. In words: information gain is how many bits of uncertainty a question removes. The tree simply asks the question with the biggest information gain, then repeats on each child.

Let's compute it by hand on a tiny example. Suppose 10 customers, 5 churned and 5 stayed — a maximally uncertain parent (1 bit). We test the split "Did the customer complain?":

In [26]:
def information_gain(parent, left, right):
    # Bits of uncertainty removed by splitting `parent` into `left` and `right`.
    n = len(parent)
    w_left, w_right = len(left) / n, len(right) / n
    after = w_left * entropy(left) + w_right * entropy(right)
    return entropy(parent) - after

# 10 customers: 5 churn, 5 stay.
parent = ["churn"] * 5 + ["stay"] * 5

# Split on "complained?": complainers are almost all churners; non-complainers almost all stay.
complained     = ["churn"] * 4 + ["stay"] * 1     # 5 who complained
not_complained = ["churn"] * 1 + ["stay"] * 4     # 5 who did not

print("parent entropy         :", round(entropy(parent), 3), "bits")
print("left  (complained)     :", round(entropy(complained), 3), "bits")
print("right (did not)        :", round(entropy(not_complained), 3), "bits")
print("-> Information Gain     :", round(information_gain(parent, complained, not_complained), 3), "bits")

parent entropy         : 1.0 bits
left  (complained)     : 0.722 bits
right (did not)        : 0.722 bits
-> Information Gain     : 0.278 bits

The split removed a big chunk of uncertainty, so "Did the customer complain?" is a good question — a tree would love it. A useless split (one that leaves both groups just as mixed as before) would score an information gain near zero.

One alternative worth knowing: Gini impurity. scikit-learn's default split criterion is not entropy but Gini impurity, $\;G = 1 - \sum_i p_i^2$. It measures the same thing — how mixed a group is — and almost always produces near-identical trees. Entropy and Gini are two rulers for the same quantity; use whichever you like.

In [27]:
def gini(labels):
    # Gini impurity: another measure of how "mixed" a set of labels is.
    labels = np.asarray(labels)
    _, counts = np.unique(labels, return_counts=True)
    p = counts / counts.sum()
    return float(1 - np.sum(p ** 2))

for group, name in [(urn_a, "Urn A (8:2)"), (urn_b, "Urn B (5:5)")]:
    print(f"{name:12s}  entropy = {entropy(group):.3f} bits   gini = {gini(group):.3f}")

8.9 Overfitting — and how to tame it¶

A tree left to grow without limits will keep splitting until every leaf is perfectly pure — it will memorise the training data, quirks and noise included. That is overfitting: dazzling on data it has seen, unreliable on data it has not. The tell-tale sign is a big gap between training accuracy and test accuracy.

Let's grow an unrestricted tree and watch the gap appear:

In [36]:
deep = DecisionTreeClassifier(criterion="entropy", random_state=42)   # no depth limit
deep.fit(X_train, y_train)
print(f"Unlimited tree — train accuracy: {accuracy_score(y_train, deep.predict(X_train)):.1%}")
print(f"Unlimited tree — test  accuracy: {accuracy_score(y_test,  deep.predict(X_test)):.1%}")
print(f"depth actually reached: {deep.get_depth()},  leaves: {deep.get_n_leaves()}")

Unlimited tree — train accuracy: 99.3%
Unlimited tree — test  accuracy: 94.9%
depth actually reached: 16,  leaves: 97

Near-perfect on training data, noticeably worse on test data — the classic overfitting fingerprint. The cure is to limit the tree's complexity. The main dials:

  • max_depth — how many questions deep the tree may go.
  • min_samples_leaf — refuse to create a leaf with too few customers in it.
  • ccp_alpha — cost-complexity pruning: snip back branches that do not earn their keep.

The right depth is the one that does best on the test set. Let's sweep max_depth and plot both curves — the sweet spot is where the test line peaks, before the tree starts memorising:

In [37]:
depths = range(1, 16)
train_acc, test_acc = [], []
for d in depths:
    m = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=d, criterion="entropy", random_state=42).fit(X_train, y_train)
    train_acc.append(accuracy_score(y_train, m.predict(X_train)))
    test_acc.append(accuracy_score(y_test,  m.predict(X_test)))

plt.plot(list(depths), train_acc, "-o", label="training accuracy", color="#d62728")
plt.plot(list(depths), test_acc,  "-o", label="test accuracy",     color="#2ca02c")
plt.axvline(int(np.argmax(test_acc)) + 1, ls="--", color="grey", lw=1, label="best test depth")
plt.xlabel("max_depth"); plt.ylabel("accuracy")
plt.title("Deeper is not better: the test curve peaks, then overfitting sets in")
plt.legend(); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

print("Best max_depth by test accuracy:", int(np.argmax(test_acc)) + 1)

8.10 Regression trees — predicting a number¶

Everything so far predicted a category (survived/died, churn/stay). But trees can just as easily predict a number — a price, a demand, a score. That is a regression tree, and only two things change:

  1. A leaf predicts the average of the training values that land in it (instead of a majority class).
  2. The split criterion is no longer entropy. A number has no "classes" to be pure about, so instead we ask each split to reduce the spread of the values — measured by variance (mean squared error). A good split produces child groups whose values are tightly clustered. scikit-learn calls this criterion="squared_error".

The idea is identical to information gain: pick the question that removes the most uncertainty — we just measure uncertainty as variance rather than entropy.

Let's make "reduce the spread" concrete, exactly as we did for information gain in §8.6 — but with numbers instead of classes. We measure a group's spread with its SSE (the average squared distance from the group's mean — this is just the variance), and a split's variance reduction is the drop in SSE, weighted by group size:

$$\text{Variance reduction} = \underbrace{\text{SSE}(\text{parent})}_{\text{spread before}} \;-\; \underbrace{\left[\, w_L\,\text{SSE}(\text{left}) + w_R\,\text{SSE}(\text{right}) \,\right]}_{\text{average spread after}}$$

It is the same formula as information gain, with SSE in place of entropy. Suppose 6 wines with quality scores [4, 4, 5, 7, 8, 8], and a candidate split that sends the three low scores one way and the three high scores the other:

In [38]:
def sse(values):
    # Regression "impurity": the average squared distance from the group's mean (its variance).
    values = np.asarray(values, dtype=float)
    return float(np.mean((values - values.mean()) ** 2))

def variance_reduction(parent, left, right):
    # The regression twin of information_gain: how much a split shrinks the spread.
    n = len(parent)
    after = (len(left) / n) * sse(left) + (len(right) / n) * sse(right)
    return sse(parent) - after

parent = [4, 4, 5, 7, 8, 8]
low    = [4, 4, 5]     # one child group
high   = [7, 8, 8]     # the other child group

print("parent SSE (spread before) :", round(sse(parent), 3))
print("left   SSE (low scores)     :", round(sse(low), 3))
print("right  SSE (high scores)    :", round(sse(high), 3))
print("-> Variance reduction        :", round(variance_reduction(parent, low, high), 3))

parent SSE (spread before) : 3.0
left   SSE (low scores)     : 0.222
right  SSE (high scores)    : 0.222
-> Variance reduction        : 2.778

The split collapses the spread — each child is tightly clustered — so this is a good regression split, exactly as a large information gain marked a good classification split. Line them up side by side and the symmetry is the whole lesson:

Classification tree Regression tree
Measures a group's "mess" with entropy (or Gini) SSE (variance)
Picks the split that maximises information gain variance reduction
A leaf predicts the majority class the average value

And that last row is the final piece: a regression leaf simply predicts the mean of its group — here, about 4.33 for the low leaf and 7.67 for the high one. That is all a regression tree ever does, all the way down: keep splitting to shrink the spread, then predict the average at each leaf.

This model predicts which of your 3,150 customers are likely to leave your service, achieving 90.7% accuracy overall. However, because only 15.7% of customers actually churn, the model's practical performance on actual churners may be lower than this headline number suggests—it could be overestimating how many at-risk customers it catches. Model Card — Customer Churn Decision Tree¶

  • Intended use. Flag customers at high risk of churning so the retention team can prioritise outreach, using rules the team can read and audit.
  • Task. Classification — churn vs stay, on 3,150 customers (~16% churn).
  • Headline metric. ~91% accuracy on a held-out test set; read alongside the confusion matrix, because the classes are imbalanced.
  • Key drivers (from feature_importances_): the features the tree relied on most — e.g. complaints, seconds of use, customer value.
  • Decision policy. A depth-3 tree yields a handful of plain-English if-then rules (see §8.12) the team can follow directly.
  • Limitations. A single tree can overfit (§8.9) and is sensitive to small data changes; accuracy is not the whole story under class imbalance; the data is historical and one telecom's — patterns may drift.
  • Monitoring. Re-check test accuracy and the churn rate monthly; retrain if either drifts materially.
